# State of the Network — 28 August 2026

## Purpose

This note records the first evidence update for the Canal Commons research programme. It is intentionally conservative: reported facts are separated from implications and hypotheses.

## 1. Governance and funding model

The Canal & River Trust (CRT) was formed in July 2012 as an independent charity taking stewardship of about 2,000 miles of waterways in England and Wales from British Waterways. Defra says the model was intended to give CRT greater freedom to generate commercial income and attract charitable income, donations and volunteers. The current government grant runs to March 2027; the government review concluded that more than £400m of new grant funding would be provided between 2027 and 2037 while reducing reliance on government funding over time.

## 2. Current financial scale

Charity Commission data for the year ended 31 March 2025 reports CRT total income of £232.6m and expenditure of £233.4m. Reported income included £100.5m from other trading activities, £74.3m from charitable activities, £50.6m from investments and £6.7m from donations and legacies. The Charity Commission record reports £62.9m of government grant income for that year.

CRT says more than half of its income now comes from property, investments, utilities, donations and other funding, and that it has an ambition to grow voluntary income tenfold over the next decade.

## 3. Maintenance and resilience

CRT reported more than £60m of winter maintenance investment across more than 200 projects in 2024/25, including 135 new lock gates and major inspections/repairs to bridges, tunnels and wash walls. A further £21m was spent upgrading 22 of its 74 large raised reservoirs. CRT also reported nearly three-quarters of a million volunteer hours.

This means the correct starting assumption is NOT that the network is simply being abandoned. A large maintenance programme continues. The strategic question is whether the scale and composition of investment is sufficient as infrastructure ages, costs rise and extreme-weather risks increase.

## 4. Llangollen breach as a resilience case study

The December 2025 Llangollen Canal breach near Whitchurch is a useful case study for hidden infrastructure risk. CRT's interim findings indicate that a long-term, deep-seated leak beneath the canal bed, combined with a steep high embankment predominantly constructed of sand, was the likely combination of factors. The leak was described as undetectable through the existing inspection regime. CRT's published repair estimate is in the order of £4m–£6m, including pumping costs, with repair work expected to take about 40 weeks from the relevant stage of construction planning.

As of August 2026, CRT reports that reconstruction remains underway; approximately 15 million litres of water had been drained from the flooded field and around 4,000 tonnes of sand removed during the recovery/repair process. The Trust says the repair is expected to cost several million pounds.

## 5. Wider navigation-authority pressure

The Environment Agency's 2025 waterways plan explicitly says its waterways service faced customer concerns, inflation and funding pressures, and set an objective of securing more income to move towards financial sustainability. It is responsible for about 1,010km of inland waterways. This suggests that the funding/resilience challenge is not unique to CRT, although the institutional models differ.

Source:
- https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/our-waterways-a-plan-for-2025

## 6. Preliminary implications for Canal Commons

### Supported by evidence
1. The UK waterways system has substantial ongoing public and charitable investment; this is not a simple abandonment story.
2. Government funding is transitioning toward lower long-term dependence on grant support.
3. CRT already has significant commercial, property and investment income, so a new organisation should not assume donations are the only missing resource.
4. Hidden infrastructure failure can create multi-million-pound emergencies.
5. Volunteer labour is already a major part of the system.
6. Other navigation authorities also face funding and service pressures.

### Hypotheses to test
1. Distributed monitoring could identify some failure modes earlier or provide better evidence for targeted investigation.
2. A transparent project-level funding marketplace could mobilise money more effectively than unrestricted appeals for some types of work.
3. A national data layer could connect existing organisations without duplicating their engineering or navigation functions.
4. A carefully selected set of commercial activities could recycle a small proportion of canal-generated economic value into infrastructure resilience.
5. Freight may be viable on selected corridors, but this must be demonstrated route-by-route rather than assumed.

## 7. Immediate research questions

- What exactly is the 2027–2037 annual grant schedule in nominal and real terms?
- What level of maintenance/resilience spending is required under different service scenarios?
- What infrastructure condition/risk data is publicly available at asset level?
- Which existing organisations already operate citizen reporting, adoption, fundraising or data platforms?
- Which canals/river navigations have credible freight opportunities today?
- Which revenue opportunities can be captured without competing destructively with CRT or other navigation authorities?
- What legal structure would best support a public-benefit organisation with future commercial activity?
